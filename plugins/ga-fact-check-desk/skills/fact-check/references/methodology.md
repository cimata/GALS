## 1. Source hierarchy

Prefer evidence in this order, while matching the source to the claim:

1. **Primary / authoritative:** legislation, judgments, regulators, official statistics, registers, filings, audited results, original studies, transcripts, official datasets, contracts or transaction documents where legitimately available.
2. **High-reliability independent secondary:** major news organisations and specialist publications with identifiable reporting and sourcing.
3. **Other reliable secondary/contextual material:** useful for leads, terminology, chronology or cross-checking.
4. **Weak / derivative:** aggregators, reposts, snippets, unattributed newsletters, social posts, forums, blogs and AI-generated summaries. Use as leads, not decisive evidence, unless the item itself is the claim being verified.

If a suitable primary source is accessible, consult it before finalising. A primary source proves what the issuing entity records or says; it does not automatically prove the entity's interpretation, motive, impact claim or promotional framing.

If the conclusion materially depends on secondary reporting because no suitable primary source is accessible, say so when it affects confidence.

## 2. Independent evidence chains

For the central material claim, seek at least two independent evidence chains where available.

Count chains, not links. These usually count as one chain:
- several outlets reproducing one wire story;
- several stories based on the same press release;
- several reports citing the same study or unnamed source;
- a corporate announcement and articles that merely repeat it.

Independence means the evidence arose through a genuinely separate source, dataset, observation, investigation or authoritative record.

A single source can be sufficient when it is uniquely authoritative for the proposition, such as an official legal text for its wording or a company's filed accounts for a reported metric. Do not invent a second source merely to satisfy a quota.

## 3. Meaning and claim scope

Before searching, determine:
- the exact proposition being asserted;
- the literal meaning;
- the reasonable reader's likely interpretation;
- the relevant time period, geography, population, category and metric;
- whether qualifiers such as "first", "only", "record", "largest", "caused", "completed", "approved" or "will" materially change the burden of proof.

A technically true sentence can still be `Engañoso` if a reasonable reading is materially false because of omitted scope, denominator, status or context.

For multi-claim statements, split claims when one component can be true while another is false. The overall verdict follows materiality, not an average of sub-verdicts.

## 4. Materiality and burden of proof

Treat the claimant as bearing the burden of proof for a factual assertion. The verifier should search diligently for confirming and disconfirming evidence, but need not prove an unlimited negative.

Classify defects internally as:
- **non-material:** does not change the substantive understanding;
- **material:** changes an important factual interpretation;
- **critical:** reverses or fundamentally alters the central claim.

A claim with several correct details can still be `Incorrecto` or `Engañoso` if the central proposition fails.

## 5. Special claim types

### Numbers and percentages
Check units, denominator, period, current vs constant prices, nominal vs real, seasonality, annualised vs period change, percentage vs percentage points, rounding and arithmetic.

### Currencies and scale
Check currency, FX date if conversion is material, million/billion/trillion conventions, and financial metric type. In Spanish, `billion` = `mil millones`; `trillion` = `billón`.

### Corporate and transactions
Distinguish proposal, talks, due diligence, non-binding offer, binding offer, signed agreement, regulatory approval and closing. Distinguish reported, organic, like-for-like, pro forma and constant-currency metrics; valuation, market capitalisation, enterprise value and transaction value.

### Law and regulation
Use the legal text, official gazette, judgment, regulator or authoritative guidance. Distinguish announced, approved, entered into force, applicable, transitional and enforceable. Guidance or interpretation is not automatically a binding legal obligation.

### Forecasts and scenarios
Preserve issuer, publication date, horizon and uncertainty. A forecast remains a forecast even if later data are consistent with it.

### Causality and motive
Do not infer causality from sequence or correlation. Distinguish demonstrated causation, attributed causation and inference. Do not infer intent, motivation or strategy without direct support.

### Rankings, records and superlatives
Check the full relevant universe, indicator, period, method and comparable historical series. A counterexample can invalidate an absolute claim.

### Quotes and attribution
Verify wording, speaker, date, context and whether a paraphrase has been turned into a direct quotation. Verify that a statement exists separately from whether its contents are true.

### Surveys and studies
Check population, sample, representativeness, methodology, fieldwork period, margin of error where applicable, funding, preprint/publication status and whether conclusions support causal language.

### Images, screenshots, charts and tables
Check provenance, date, context, axes, units, legend, source dataset and whether the visual is being interpreted beyond what it demonstrates.

## 6. Experts and interested parties

When relying materially on an expert:
- confirm expertise relevant to the precise question;
- identify institutional affiliation;
- note material financial, professional or advocacy interests when they affect interpretation;
- prefer evidence and methods over authority alone.

A company, government, campaign, claimant or accused party is a valid source for what it says or records, but its denial or assertion is not independent proof of the disputed proposition.

When feasible and proportionate, the original claimant or materially affected party may be asked for supporting evidence or comment. Their response is evidence to assess, not the verdict.

## 7. Reproducibility and preservation

Make the material path to the verdict inspectable:
- link the decisive evidence;
- identify the document/article/dataset and date;
- explain what each key source establishes when not obvious;
- distinguish direct access from a snippet, quotation or secondary description;
- preserve a stable copy, archive reference or screenshot when material evidence is likely to change or disappear, where tools and permissions allow.

Do not overload the answer with every search result. Surface only evidence that materially supports the verdict.

## 8. Corrections and later versions

Check for corrected, updated, superseded, retracted or revised sources. Use the latest authoritative version when appropriate while distinguishing:
- an original error;
- information that was accurate at the time but later changed;
- a later update that does not invalidate the earlier claim.

If maintaining a persistent fact-check record, distinguish `correction` from `update` and preserve a visible audit trail for material changes.

## 9. Stop rule

Stop when:
- the evidence is sufficient for a defensible verdict;
- additional searching is unlikely to change the conclusion materially; or
- the claim remains genuinely unresolved after reasonable effort.

Do not search indefinitely to manufacture certainty. Use `No verificable` when the evidentiary threshold is not met.

## 10. Methodological provenance

This methodology adapts recurring professional principles from public fact-checking standards and methodologies, especially:
- International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN): transparency and reproducibility of sources; best available primary evidence.
- European Fact-Checking Standards Network (EFCSN): evidence for pertinent factual statements, at least two sources for central claims when available, primary-source preference, expert credibility, independent editing and corrections.
- AFP Fact Check: independent corroboration, transparent evidence and caution when evidence is insufficient.
- Full Fact: exact wording, context and reader-verifiable sourcing.
- PolitiFact: burden of proof, exact wording, context and alternative reasonable readings.

These organisations are methodological references, not authorities whose verdicts should be copied. Verify the underlying evidence independently whenever feasible.
